What we do in this lesson

The rhythm is the same as every week. This is what fills it today.

  1. Board opening — the Executive Board takes office. Each manager gets thirty seconds: department, roles, and one thing you expect from the Board. The five council sentences are read out, the FreshWay Working Agreement is signed, and it is recorded as Board Decision #1
  2. Masterclass — what logistics actually is, its four sub-sections, and the line that runs from data to a decision.
  3. The five bodies meet — the Management Team and the four councils, at the same time. In the councils you do the Research Question Exchange: you leave with one question you can only answer with data held by another department.
  4. Board close — your manager says out loud what your department will have ready by lesson 3.

Two new instruments

The Board is in office from today, so the two instruments that hang on it open as well. You do not need either of them this week; you need to know they exist.

Information Request Non-Delivery Notice

A written request to the Executive Board for something that is not published anywhere. Some FreshWay material is sensitive or strategic and is deliberately not on Brightspace — it exists, and you have to ask for it.

You get an answer within two working days, and each department may file at most two per lesson. So ask before you need it in a council meeting, not during. The Board archive index — the list of what exists but is not published — opens at the end of this lesson.

A short notice to the Board saying that another department did not deliver what was agreed. It points to a dated line in the Management Team minutes, so it is a fact and not an accusation.

It exists so that a department that is blocked can carry on and say so, instead of quietly falling behind. Filing one is not a complaint about people; not filing one, and handing in nothing, is the problem.

What logistics actually is

Logistics is the organisation, planning, control and execution of the flow of goods — from development and procurement, through production and distribution, to the end customer, including everything that comes back. That last clause is easy to skip and expensive to forget: returned parcels, pallets, crates, packaging and waste are logistics too, and at a supermarket they run every day.

Two things follow from that definition. First, logistics is not always physical: hospitals, banks, insurers and schools run administrative logistics — flows of files, claims and patients organised in exactly the same way. Second, physical logistics is conventionally split into four sub-sections. That split is the classification the National Platform for Logistics (LPL) uses for Dutch higher education, and it is the one we use here.

Table 1. The four sub-sections of logistics, and where they sit at FreshWay

Transport

Moving goods along the chain. Five modalities: road, rail, water, air and pipeline. Not only goods move — energy, information and money do too.

Two things about that last column are worth noticing before you defend your own department. FreshWay has no production logistics of its own, which means everything upstream of the distribution centre is somebody else's process that you can only influence by agreement. And E-commerce sits awkwardly: it promises the customer something, but transport and warehousing have to deliver it. Most of what goes wrong in logistics goes wrong at the seams between these four, not inside them.

The Netherlands and logistics

Logistics is a large part of the Dutch economy: a gateway position, the Port of Rotterdam and Schiphol, and a dense network of roads, rail, water, energy and data. For years the story was expansion — the country as the warehouse of North-West Europe, bursting at the seams.

That story has turned. Vacancy in logistics property is now rising in several regions, and the trade press writes about building for vacancy. What has not turned is the labour shortage: the sector expects to need around 350,000 new employees by 2030.

Both claims come from the same kind of source, a few years apart, and they point in opposite directions. That is the reason every claim in your report carries a date. It is also the cheapest possible argument for the second of the three questions from lesson 1: when?

From data to a decision

Business intelligence is the narrow, useful version of a very broad term: you collect data from inside the company and from its environment, you turn it into information, and you present it so clearly that it leads to an action or a decision. The word to hold on to is decision. A dashboard that changes nothing anyone does is decoration.

The route from one to the other has four rungs. It is worth walking them slowly once, because almost every weak advice report in this course fails on the same rung.

  1. Data — what is recorded. Every truck arriving at the distribution centre is scanned at the gate: plate, door, timestamp. On its own this tells you nothing.
  2. Information — data with a question put to it. Compare those timestamps with the booked slots and you know how many trucks arrived outside their slot last week.
  3. Knowledge — information with context around it. Put that number next to the supplier, the product group and the day of the week, and a pattern appears: it is not all suppliers, it is a few, on particular days.
  4. Wisdom — knowledge someone acts on. Move those suppliers' slots, or change the agreement with them — and accept that you are not going to fix the rest this year.

In a company, wisdom has a plain name: the handful of numbers a department steers on, its KPIs — key performance indicators. Your department file already lists yours, so you are not starting from a blank page. Choosing three KPIs means deciding what you will not measure, and that is a decision you have to be able to defend, not a formality.

Watch out for this

The most common wrong turn in this course is to start at rung one: look at the data you have and see what comes out. It produces a lot of charts and no advice. Start at the top instead — name the decision someone at FreshWay has to make, then ask which numbers would change that decision. More data is not more information.

  • FreshWay glossary — twenty words, in Company info > About FreshWay. Every term used in the lessons, on one page. Look it up rather than guess — a case, a colli and a roll container are not the same thing.
  • Company dossier, same folder. The figures behind FreshWay, including the one that decides this course: seventeen million euro a year to invest, and seven departments asking for it.
  • Your own department file. It names the KPIs your department is judged on. Read them before the lesson; you will need them in the councils.
