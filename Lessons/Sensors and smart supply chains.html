Lesson 7 — Sensors and smart supply chains

A sensor is easy to add to a plan and hard to justify. This lesson gives you the one test that separates a sensor plan from a shopping list: name the measurement, name who uses it, and name the number it changes.

Before you come

  • Hand in SA5 — self-organising logistics, including its impact map and its Cross-delivery paragraph. It has to be in before the lesson starts, because the Board reads it before the commitments review.
  • Confirm the handed-over decision you received from another department: one line saying you got it and whether you accept the limits they set.
  • Read the section Read this before the lesson below. About eight minutes — there is one concept this week, not two.
  • Bring a laptop, and have your own department's data open. You will be looking for the columns that already come from a sensor.

What we do in this lesson

The rhythm is back to normal after the Interim Board Review. This is what fills it today.

  1. Board opening and commitments — the Board walks through the Decision Log, each manager answers for last week's commitment, and you get feedback on the impact maps. Two departments present theirs.
  2. Masterclass — sensor and IoT applications and what they are used for; real-time visibility and track & trace; bio- and nanosensors in the cold chain; smart access policies, geofencing and digital permits; and smart supply chains, including distributed ledger and why the best-known platform closed with its technology working.
  3. The five bodies meet — in the Data Council you arrange this week's Data Consumer Confirmation: the department that will actually use your sensor data, and the KPI they say it moves.
  4. Department work — you make a shortlist of sensor applications that are plausible for your department, and then fill in the Sensor application canvas for one of them.
  5. Board close — your manager says out loud what your department will have ready by lesson 8.

The Sensor application canvas is in Company info > Forms and templates. It is a working form, not an extra assignment: it asks you, in order, what is measured, where, how often, who uses it, and what changes because of it. Open it once before the lesson so you know what it will ask you.

Read this before the lesson

What a sensor actually measures, and who the consumer is

Start with the modest version of the truth, because almost every overclaim in this lesson begins by skipping it. A sensor measures one quantity, at one place, at one moment. That is all it does. Everything after that — that the load is too warm, that the truck has arrived, that the machine is about to fail — is interpretation. Interpretation is where the value comes from, and it is also where the error comes from.

The difference matters because a report that confuses the two cannot be checked. Three examples, all of them things FreshWay already records.

Table 1. What is measured, what may be concluded, and what may not
The measurement What you may conclude What you may not conclude
Temperature at goods receipt

The air at the probe was this warm at that moment.

That the core temperature of the product was the same, or that it stayed within range during the whole journey. One reading is not a history.
Plate recognition at the gate

This registration was seen by this camera at this time.

That the right truck arrived with the right load. A dirty plate, poor lighting or a swapped trailer all break the link.
Crate temperature on a delivery route

This crate was this warm when it was scanned.

That the customer received a product that was safe, or unsafe. That depends on how long, how often and how far outside range — not on one number.

Now the second half, and it is the one that decides your grade. Sensor data without a consumer is a cost item. Every sensor has to be bought, fitted, powered, connected, calibrated and maintained, and it produces a stream of numbers that someone has to store and look after. None of that pays for itself by existing. It pays for itself when a named person or system does something differently because of what was measured.

So the question the sub-assignment runs on is not which sensors could we fit? — you could fit hundreds — but this one: which KPI changes because of this measurement, and whose KPI is it? Two things follow from asking it that way. First, the consumer is very often not your own department: a measurement at goods receipt changes what Storage and Inventory can do, and a crate temperature on a delivery route says something about how E-commerce should have set the slot. Second, if you cannot name the consumer, you have not found a weak application — you have found no application.

That is also why this week's cross-delivery is what it is. You do not confirm your own sensor plan; the department that will use the data does, and they name the KPI. It is a deliberately hard confirmation to get, and getting it is most of the work.

Watch out for this

Biosensors are the place where this course is overclaimed hardest, so be precise about where they work. Biosensors using DNA probes, CRISPR/Cas systems and immunoassays can identify E. coli, Salmonella and Listeria, and they are being built into portable devices that give a result in minutes. That is real, and it is useful exactly where FreshWay would want it: at quality control and at incoming goods, where a result in minutes still allows you to intervene.

What does not exist yet is a sensor in consumer packaging that tells a shopper whether a product is contaminated. Peer-reviewed reviews name four unresolved barriers: interference from the food matrix itself, instability of the biological recognition elements, manufacturing at scale, and the absence of regulatory standardisation. So place this technology at quality control and goods receipt in your plan — not on the shelf.

  • Sensor application canvas, in Company info > Forms and templates. The form SA6 is delivered on. Read it before the lesson; it is faster to fill in when you already know the order of the questions.
  • Your own department's data. Several columns you already have are sensor readings under another name — a temperature at arrival, a gate waiting time, a crate temperature on a route. Starting from a measurement that already exists is usually a stronger proposal than starting from one that has to be bought.
  • Peer-reviewed reviews on pathogen detection — in Foods (MDPI, December 2025) and Food Bioengineering (Wiley, 2025). These are the sources behind the maturity claim above; cite them rather than a vendor page if you use this technology.
  • FreshWay glossary — twenty words, in Company info > About FreshWay. Temperature zone, on time and KPI are the three you will need this week.

What you agree at the end of this lesson

  • SA6 — sensor plan, handed in on Brightspace before lesson 8. Which sensor applications are feasible for your department, with credible sources for what the technology can actually do today — and then the Sensor application canvas filled in for one of them, worked out properly.
  • Inside it, one Cross-delivery paragraph: a Data Consumer Confirmation. Your sensor data has a named consumer in another department, and that department states which KPI the measurement changes for them. They confirm it in one line. A sensor without a confirmed consumer does not go in the plan.

Adjusting a commitment in advance costs nothing: say before the lesson what you will not manage and what you will deliver instead. Saying nothing is the only real failure.
