The systems a supermarket runs on

A supermarket does not have one system. It has a handful of large ones, each responsible for a piece of the operation, and every one of them leaves data behind. You do not have to be able to build them — you have to know which one your numbers come out of, because that decides what the numbers mean.

Table 1. The core systems, and the data each one leaves behind
System What it does The data it creates
ERP

The company's administrative backbone: purchasing, stock, sales, finance, staff. Everything that has to be booked somewhere.

Orders, invoices, stock positions, cost bookings.
WMS

Runs the distribution centre: goods in, put-away, order picking, goods out, returns.

Receipts, storage locations, pick lines, picking times, errors.
TMS

Plans transport: which load on which vehicle, in which order, along which route.

Routes, stops, kilometres, planned and actual arrival times.
POS

The checkout. It sells, and in doing so it says what actually left the shelf.

Sales per product, per store, per moment — the only true demand signal there is.
E-business platform

The webshop: the customer chooses a basket and a delivery slot, and the promise is made there.

Online orders, chosen slots, substitutions, customer communication.

Those five are inside the company. The link outwards has its own name: EDI, electronic data interchange — a fixed message format in which a supplier's system talks to yours without anyone retyping anything. An order goes out, an electronic despatch advice comes back saying what is actually on the truck, and the goods receipt can be checked against it automatically.

That is the theory. At FreshWay there are 450 suppliers, and roughly a third of them send no electronic despatch advice at all — you can see it yourself in the edi_desadv column of suppliers.csv. For that third, everything that arrives has to be counted and typed in by hand at the dock. A system is only as good as the least digital partner connected to it, and that is a business problem long before it is an IT problem.

What a data flow diagram is

A data flow diagram is a picture of how data moves through an organisation: where it enters, what happens to it, where it is kept, and where it leaves. It deliberately says nothing about technology — no servers, no databases, no programming. That is why a manager can read one, and it is why we use it here. It has exactly four elements: an actor (someone or something outside the process that sends or receives data), a process (something that happens to the data), a data store (where the data sits still), and a data flow (the arrow, with a name on it).

Three steps of a real FreshWay flow, to see the four elements in place.

  1. Actor to process — a supplier sends an electronic despatch advice: this truck, these product groups, this many pallets, this slot. The supplier is an actor; the message is a data flow, and its name is despatch advice.
  2. Process to data store — at the goods receipt the truck is actually weighed, counted and temperature-checked, and the result is compared with what was announced. That comparison is the process. What comes out of it is written to the receipts record — the data store you know as inbound_receipts.csv.
  3. Data store to actor — Inventory and supply chain management reads that record to know what is really in stock. Another department, another arrow, and the reason your diagram cannot stop at your own door.

Two rules keep a diagram readable. Every arrow has a name, because data is not a name. And every arrow starts and ends somewhere: no loose ends, no flows that come from nowhere. Where your arrow crosses into another department, that is exactly where an Interface Agreement belongs.

You have three files, not seventeen

The FreshWay dataset as a whole is large. Your part of it is not. Your department folder holds at most three files, plus the shared master data everybody gets — stores, product groups, vehicles, suppliers and sites — and a data_dictionary.csv that explains every column of every file, with its unit and its meaning.

All of it covers one quarter: Monday 14 October 2024 to Sunday 12 January 2025, thirteen weeks. That window is not an average quarter. It contains the busiest week of the year and the quietest, four weeks apart. Take that into account before you extrapolate anything from it.

Watch out for this

A sample is not a total. Some files are a sample on purpose, and they say so in a column. The pick lines are a systematic one-in-twelve sample, with the fraction written in sample_fraction; multiply by one divided by that fraction and you are back at the real volume. The two home-delivery order files cover one delivery region, about a fifth of national orders — for anything national, use the daily file that has all six sites in it. Divide by the wrong denominator and every conclusion after it is wrong by the same factor.

And empty is not zero. An empty cell means not recorded, which is a different fact from nothing happened. A spreadsheet will happily average the two together. The Dataset guide lists the traps per file — read the two pages about your own file before you draw a conclusion from it.

  • Your first five minutes with your data, in Company info > About FreshWay. One page, four movements and one habit: open · filter · group · join — and check. Worked out on the same two files as the demonstration, so you can do it again afterwards at your own pace.
  • Dataset guide, same folder. What is in each file and, more importantly, which trap is in it. This is the one document that will save you a rewrite.
  • data_dictionary.csv, next to every dataset. Every column, with type, unit and meaning. Look a column up rather than guess what it holds.
  • FreshWay glossary — twenty words, same folder. Case, pick line, temperature zone, fill rate, on time. The column names of your data are in there too.

What you agree at the end of this lesson
