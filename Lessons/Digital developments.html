Read this before the lesson

Standard, interoperability and dataspace, in plain language

Three words do most of the work in this lesson, and they are usually used as if they meant the same thing. They do not.

A standard is an agreement about form. It says what a message looks like — which fields it has, in which order, in which format — so that a system that has never met yours can still read what you send. EDI is a standard. So is the electronic freight document that authorities across the EU will have to accept from July 2027.

Interoperability is whether two organisations actually do it. A standard on paper that nobody has implemented buys you nothing; interoperability is the standard plus the connection plus the willingness. It is the difference between could exchange data and does exchange data.

A dataspace is the third step: a set of agreements that lets organisations share data without losing control over it. Who may see what, for how long, for which purpose, and how that is enforced. It exists because at this level the barrier is no longer technical but a matter of trust — nobody wants to hand a competitor a copy of their operation.

FreshWay is a good place to see all three at once. The standard exists, and has for decades. Yet a third of the 450 suppliers still send no electronic despatch advice, so for that third there is no interoperability — and a shared dataspace with competing retailers is not even on the table. The technical barrier is almost always the smaller of the two. A platform can close down with its technology working perfectly, simply because too few competitors were willing to join it.

Is it running, or has it been announced?

This is the question SA3 stands or falls on. Every technology you will read about this week comes in three flavours, and press coverage does not distinguish between them: it is in production (real users, real volume, today), it is a pilot (it works in a controlled setting, at small scale), or it is a press release (it has been announced, or it is a target for a future year). All three can be true and interesting. Only the first one belongs in a business case without a caveat.

Three questions get you there, and you can usually answer them in ten minutes.

  1. Who uses it? Name the organisation. A named customer is a fact; “leading retailers” is marketing.
  2. Since when? A date, and then the follow-up: is it still running? A technology can be announced in one year and quietly withdrawn the next.
  3. How much? How many sites, how many orders, how many vehicles. Scale is what separates a pilot from an operation.

You will need those questions this week, because the deck itself gives you three cases where the honest answer is uncomfortable. Humanoid robots in warehouses are pilots and demonstrations, not deployed technology — you cannot buy one and put it in a plan. Federated learning is in production in healthcare, banking and mobile devices, but in logistics it is still simulation and pilot; the most-cited study of a pan-European truck network is explicitly a simulated deployment. And the striking figures around the Dutch dataspace programme — the number of SMEs connected, the share of Rotterdam container volume — are targets for 2027, published as intended results. Ambition is not adoption.

None of that makes these developments a weak subject. It makes them a better one. Arguing why a sector with an obvious data-sharing problem has not adopted the technique built for exactly that problem is a stronger piece of advice than repeating that the technique exists.

The foundation underneath

A 2025 survey by evofenedex among about 300 professionals in Dutch trade and logistics found that 60% are reasonably to very interested in AI, while only 7% have a concrete AI policy. Only 42% have their internal data largely in order; for a third of them it is limited.

The same study warns against AI-washing: presenting every new application as smart and intelligent while its added value is limited. That warning cuts both ways — it applies to the vendors you read, and to the report you write.

Note what that source is and is not. It is an industry survey of roughly 300 people, dated 2025 — not national statistics. Saying so costs you one sentence and makes the figure usable. That is exactly the label your own sources need.

Watch out for this

A forecast is not a result, and it does not become one by being repeated. Last year this course showed a slide saying that federated learning pilots were “expected to scale”. A year later they have not. The slide was not wrong to make the prediction; it would have been wrong to copy it forward without checking.

So when you take over a claim about the future, write down when it was made and by whom, and then look for what happened since. Do not repeat a forecast — check it.
