Lesson 5 — AI and GenAI in logistics

This is the lesson in which it is easiest to promise too much. You get one test that tells an AI agent apart from ordinary automation, and one question that decides whether your advice is a business case or a brochure.

Before you come

  • Confirm the Technology Register entry you were cited in or that you took from: one line saying you got it and whether it was usable.
  • Read the section Read this before the lesson below. About ten minutes.

What we do in this lesson

The rhythm is the same as every week. This is what fills it today.

  1. Board opening and commitments — the Board walks through the Decision Log, each manager answers for last week's commitment, and you get feedback on SA3. One department presents, chosen on the day.
  2. Masterclass — the current developments in AI, with agentic AI treated on its own because it is the only one with both a production track record and a documented failure rate. Then six documented cases side by side: what is genuinely running, what was rolled back and why, and two claims about the same shipping company of which only one can be traced.
  3. Exercise — you make a transport plan in Excel by hand, then upload the same file to a general-purpose AI assistant and ask it to do the same. Compare the two, and be able to say which one you would defend.
  4. The five bodies meet — and in your department work afterwards you turn what you brought back into the AI Impact Note.
  5. Board close — your manager says out loud what your department will have ready by lesson 6.

Read this before the lesson

An agent, or automation with a new label?

An AI agent is a system that decides its own next step. Not if this, then that, written out in advance by a person, but a system that is given a goal and works out how to reach it. There is a workable test for it: does the system independently re-sequence its objectives, weigh trade-offs against each other, and adapt the way it works when circumstances change? If the answer is no, it is conventional automation.

Conventional automation is very often the right answer — it is cheaper, more predictable and easier to explain to an auditor. But then price it as automation and claim what automation delivers. Selling it as something more has a name in the trade press: agent washing. It is worth recognising, because you will read a lot of it this week.

Two sentences belong in the same breath, because they save more advice reports than any technology will. First, a prediction is a number with an error margin. Your own weekly data carries the margin in a column called forecast_error_pct; a forecast quoted without it is half a number. Second, a great many logistics questions are already answered by grouping and adding up. Before you propose a model, ask the honest question: would a pivot table have solved this? If it would, say so — that is a finding, not a failure.

Which KPI would show that it is going wrong?

In 2024 the payments company Klarna largely replaced its customer service with AI: 2.3 million conversations in the first month, roughly the workload of 700 staff, and a reported saving of around forty million dollars a year. In May 2025 its chief executive said publicly that the company had gone too far, and it began hiring people again. It now runs a hybrid model — AI for routine questions, people for escalations.

The interesting part is not that it went wrong. It is which numbers said so, and when. The volume metrics stayed green the entire time: conversations handled, handling time, cost per conversation, all improving. The damage showed up somewhere else and later — in satisfaction and in the score customers gave afterwards. If you had been watching the dashboard that was built to prove the project a success, you would have seen nothing at all.

So name two KPIs, always, for every solution you propose. The one you expect to improve — that is the easy one, and everyone writes it down. And the one that would deteriorate first if this went wrong — and then check that FreshWay is actually measuring it, at a frequency that would let anyone notice in time. A solution whose failure is invisible is not a solution; it is a risk with a dashboard on top.

That second KPI is also the cheapest way to satisfy the rubric. Criterion 3 asks for costs, benefits and risks. A named, measured early-warning number is a risk analysis; “there are of course risks” is not.

Where these projects actually fail

Gartner expected in 2026 that more than 40% of agentic-AI projects would be cancelled before the end of 2027 — on rising costs, unclear business value and inadequate risk control. The same firm expects organisations to abandon 60% of AI projects that are not supported by AI-ready data.

Put that next to the Dutch figure from last week: only 42% of trade and logistics companies have their internal data largely in order. Two independent sources, two continents, one diagnosis. Projects do not fail on the model. They fail on integration, data access, governance, accountability, and the jump from pilot to full scale.

That is useful to you rather than discouraging. It tells you where the paragraphs in your advice have to be: not on how clever the technique is, but on whether FreshWay has the data, at the quality and frequency your solution needs.

Watch out for this

The three questions from lesson 1 — who, when, who else — count double this week, and in a way that is easy to get backwards. In the lesson you will see two claims about the same shipping company. One is beautifully specific: AI agents route a stated percentage of the global container fleet and save a stated number of millions a year in fuel. It appears on several vendor blogs, with no company source and no independent reporting behind it. The other is duller, dated, published by the company itself — and checkable.

The specific one is more quotable, and that is exactly why you should distrust it. Precision is not evidence. A number that nobody can trace is weaker than a vaguer number that somebody signed.

  • EU AI Act, Article 50. Since 2 August 2026, people must be told when they are interacting with an AI system, and an agent must disclose both that it is AI and on whose behalf it acts. That is not only a constraint on what you advise FreshWay to build — it also applies to your own department's agent team the moment its output leaves the group.
  • The six cases in the lesson deck — two that are running at scale, one that was rolled back, one that shows autonomy inside human-set limits, the failure statistics, and the pair of claims. They are on the slides rather than on this page because they only do their work side by side.
  • Your own weekly data. The forecast and the forecast error sit next to each other in the same file. Look at the size of that error before you promise an improvement on it.
  • FreshWay glossary — twenty words, in Company info > About FreshWay. Forecast, forecast error and KPI are all in there.
