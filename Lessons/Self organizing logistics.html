Lesson 6 — Self organising logistics

Self-organising logistics sounds like a concept until you have to point at one decision you would actually hand over. That is what this lesson asks of you — and today the whole company sits in one room to hear how far each department has got.

What we do in this lesson

Two blocks are different this week. Everything else runs as usual.

  1. Board opening and commitments — the Board walks through the Decision Log, each manager answers for last week's commitment, and you get feedback on SA4.
  2. Masterclass — self-organising logistics: swarm robotics, digital twins and agentic systems, closed-loop control, collaborative logistics and asset sharing, energy-aware logistics and smart grids, and the city-edge distribution hub. With the cases, including two announcements by the same company one year apart that ended in opposite ways.
  3. Interim Board Review — instead of the five bodies, and taking part of the department work as well. Plenary, with both members of the Board. Each department is asked the same things: what have you actually got, what are you still missing, who is it with, and what have you promised the other departments that has not landed yet. The Board asks follow-up questions and records what it decides in the Decision Log.
  4. Department work — shorter than usual, and with one job: choose the development that fits your department and draw the impact map for it on a flipchart.
  5. Board close — your manager says out loud what your department will have ready by lesson 7.

The impact map

The impact map is the form in which SA5 is handed in, so it is worth knowing before you walk in. It is not new theory; it is four columns that force you to keep going one step further than the technology.

  1. Technology — what you would put in place. One thing, named.
  2. Behaviour — what people or systems then do differently. If nothing changes here, the rest of the row is empty.
  3. Effect — what that changes in the operation, measurably: fewer movements, shorter waiting time, less shrink.
  4. Value — what it is worth, and to whom: to the company, to the customer, to the environment. Those three do not always point the same way, and saying so is part of the answer.

What it means to hand over a decision

There is a line that runs straight through this whole course, and it sits between two kinds of system. One advises: it works something out and puts it in front of a person, who decides. The other decides: it acts, and the person finds out afterwards — if at all. The technology on both sides can be identical. What differs is who is accountable when it is wrong, and that is a management question, not a technical one.

The most usable middle form is worth copying, and you saw it last week. Walmart's agents keep an integrated stock picture across stores and fulfilment centres, flag disruptions and adjust order quantities themselves — including prices, but only within margin limits approved in advance by a human. That is what human oversight looks like when it actually works: not a person clicking “approve” four hundred times a day, and not a system left to its own devices, but a band that somebody set, deliberately, and can narrow again. It is also roughly what the EU AI Act will require of high-risk systems from December 2027.

Which means the design question is never shall we automate this? but three sharper ones: which decision do we hand over, within which limits, and how do we get it back? Write that third one down too. A system you cannot switch off without stopping the operation has not been handed a decision — it has been handed the company.

And then the boundary. Some decisions you do not hand over, whatever the business case says: the ones with a safety consequence, the ones with a legal consequence, and the ones you would not be able to explain to a customer or a regulator afterwards. If you cannot explain how the decision was reached, you cannot answer for it — and you are still the one who has to.

Zero-emission zones and grid congestion, in five sentences

Two physical constraints decide whether a Dutch city-logistics plan can be carried out at all, and neither of them is negotiable by a good business case.

The first: 21 zero-emission zones for freight transport are now in force, and more follow — a zone at Schiphol since January 2026, the Hague zone extended to the coast, Arnhem and Dordrecht from July 2026. Inside a zone you deliver emission-free, or you have an exemption. The second: large parts of the Dutch electricity grid are full, and a new or heavier connection can take years. That touches almost anything you might propose — charging electric vans and trucks, automating a distribution centre, extending cooling capacity, connecting a new hub. It is taken seriously enough that since January 2026 companies which cannot get a grid connection are a separate exemption category in the zero-emission zones.

The consequence for your design is one sentence long: energy has become a design criterion, not just a cost item. When and where you charge decides whether your plan can run at all, not only what it costs. Check the grid capacity before you promise electrification — the contracted connection and the current peak of all nine FreshWay sites are in sites.csv, and stores.csv says which stores lie inside a zero-emission zone. Both are public master data, so every department has them.

Electric replenishment: already decided

Two Dutch grocers have stopped treating this as a scenario. Lidl Netherlands states that from 2027 all its Dutch stores will be supplied by electric trucks — 180 of them, up from around 90 today. Albert Heijn already delivers and replenishes fully emission-free in four city centres, with the stated aim of doing so for all stores and customers in the Netherlands and Belgium by 2030.

Both come from company newsrooms and have been widely reported in the independent trade press — company statements, dated, checkable, and worth rechecking in 2027.

The question that turns this from policy into a business case: what did the zone cost them, and what did it buy them? Nobody electrifies a fleet only because a municipality asked.

Watch out for this

Say what size of thing you are proposing. A simulation of one warehouse zone is a project you could start next month. A digital twin of the whole chain is a multi-year programme with its own budget and its own department. Both are called a digital twin. If your report does not say which of the two it means, a reader will assume the cheap one and be disappointed later — and the Investment Council will not be able to compare your proposal with anyone else's.

The same discipline applies to the technology you cite. A swarm robot that exists in a research lab, however impressive, cannot be bought and so cannot be in a business case as available technology. A press release is not evidence: check whether something is still running before you build a plan on it.
